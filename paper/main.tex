\begin{abstract}
Do multilingual large language models (LLMs) truly ``think in English'', or is this conclusion an artifact of studying concepts that have direct English equivalents? While recent mechanistic investigations reveal that intermediate transformer representations frequently align with English, human languages are characterized by lexical gaps: culturally distinct concepts that lack single-word translations. We investigate how multilingual LLMs process these lexical gaps across four open-weight models (Llama-3-8B, Llama-3-8B-Instruct, Mistral-7B-v0.3, Qwen2.5-7B) using an audited benchmark of 212 concepts and a strictly matched subset of 41 one-to-one pairs across 10 languages (100\% matched on semantic category and script).
Our investigation yields four primary findings.
\textbf{(1)~Behavioral asymmetry.} Evaluating model generations blind to condition using a three-dimensional rubric (semantic correctness, nuance retained, cultural adequacy) demonstrates that instruction tuning imposes a selective faithfulness penalty on lexical gaps (for Llama-3-8B-Instruct, Cohen's $d = -0.26$, paired permutation $p = 0.042$), whereas uncalibrated base models produce verbose, wandering circumlocution.
\textbf{(2)~Surface confound warning.} Naive logistic regression predicting translatability purely from surface features (character length, token count, language, category) achieves 75.0\% accuracy ($\text{AUROC} = 0.834$), but collapses to chance (41.7\%, $\text{AUROC} = 0.417$) on our strictly matched subset, proving that naive interpretability probes risk measuring orthographic artifacts rather than semantic representations.
\textbf{(3)~Representational hierarchy.} Linear probes with concept-grouped cross-validation recover 10-way source language identity with near-perfect normalized improvement over chance (100\%), while translatability forms an authentic but subordinate linear direction (88--92\% normalized accuracy), exceeding the surface baseline by +52 percentage points.
\textbf{(4)~The English routing mirage.} English Routing Rate (ERR) and logit-lens English probability mass fail to diagnose lexical gaps on strictly matched pairs (all $p > 0.29$; $|d| \le 0.133$), tracking early-layer unigram vocabulary priors and tokenizer composition rather than semantic anglicization.
Together, our results show that in a controlled lexical-gap inventory, models exhibit lower cultural-explanation faithfulness in instruction-tuned models, while ERR does not provide a stable diagnostic.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Multilingual large language models (LLMs) demonstrate remarkable cross-lingual capabilities, yet mechanistic analyses of their internal representations remain overwhelmingly viewed through an English lens. A growing body of interpretability research argues that multilingual transformers route semantic processing through an English-centric latent space~\cite{schut2025think,wendler2024llamas}, and that intermediate layers leverage romanization as a conceptual pivot between non-Latin scripts and target generations~\cite{saji2025romanlens}. However, these empirical claims rely almost exclusively on parallel datasets and translation pairs where direct, one-to-one lexical equivalents exist.

This methodology leaves a fundamental linguistic question unaddressed: \emph{what happens when a concept possesses no English equivalent?} Human languages are not isomorphic encodings of a universal inventory of words; rather, they harbor profound \emph{lexical gaps}, crystallizing complex, culturally embedded phenomena into a single lexical unit~\cite{sapir1929,brown1954study}.

Consider the Japanese concept \textit{Tsundoku} (the habit of acquiring books and letting them pile up unread) versus its control counterpart \textit{Tempura} (a deep-fried dish), or the German emotion \textit{Schadenfreude} (pleasure derived from another's misfortune) versus \textit{Wanderlust} (desire to travel). When a multilingual model encounters such a concept, three distinct architectural outcomes are plausible:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Subspace Anglicization}: The model forcibly projects the representation into an English-adjacent latent vector, discarding culturally specific semantic nuances.
    \item \textbf{Representational Encapsulation}: The model isolates the concept within a distinct, language-specific manifold where untranslatable words occupy specialized geometric coordinates.
    \item \textbf{Behavioral Degradation}: The model's capacity to articulate and explain the cultural concept deteriorates, even while internal routing statistics appear indistinguishable from standard vocabulary.
\end{enumerate}

Distinguishing among these hypotheses requires navigating severe methodological hazards. In unconstrained benchmarks, untranslatable concepts differ systematically from ordinary controls in character length, subword fragmentation, and domain distribution. Indeed, a simple non-neural baseline predicts whether a concept is translatable with 75.0\% accuracy from surface orthography alone. Without strict control pairing, interpretability experiments risk measuring tokenizer artifacts rather than conceptual representations.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
In this work, we present a rigorous, tripartite evaluation of cultural lexical gaps across four open-weight models (Table~\ref{tab:models}):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Strict Bipartite Benchmark}: We introduce an audited lexical-gap inventory of 212 concepts across 10 languages and construct 41 strictly matched within-language pairs using Hungarian bipartite matching (Table~\ref{tab:matching_balance}), achieving 100\% category and script balance while collapsing surface predictability to chance (41.7\%).
    \item \textbf{Condition-Blind Behavioral Faithfulness}: We design an objective, label-invariant evaluation rubric assessing semantic correctness, nuance retained, and cultural adequacy. On strictly matched controls, instruction-tuned models exhibit a selective, statistically significant faithfulness deficit on lexical gaps (for Llama-3-8B-Instruct, Cohen's $d = -0.26$, paired permutation $p = 0.042$), whereas uncalibrated base models exhibit length-driven circumlocution.
    \item \textbf{Representational Geometry vs.\ Surface Confounds}: Probing residual streams with concept-grouped cross-validation reveals that language identity dominates latent geometry (100\% normalized improvement over chance), while translatability forms an authentic linear direction exceeding surface baselines by +52 percentage points.
    \item \textbf{The Diagnostic Mirage of English Routing}: We demonstrate that aggregate English Routing Rate (ERR) and logit-lens English mass are statistically invariant to lexical gaps on matched controls ($p > 0.29, |d| \le 0.133$), driven instead by early-layer unigram priors and tokenizer vocabulary composition.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Latent language and English-centric representations.}
A central hypothesis in multilingual mechanistic interpretability posits that transformers operate via a shared ``concept space'' structurally aligned with English~\cite{schut2025think,wendler2024llamas}. Saji et al.~\cite{saji2025romanlens} demonstrate that non-Latin representations often traverse an intermediate romanized trajectory before decoding into native scripts. However, these studies evaluate concepts with symmetric English translations. We extend this inquiry to the lexical-gap regime, examining whether this latent convergence holds when no target English equivalent exists.

\paragraph{Cultural representation and sparse features.}
Recent advances in representation engineering and sparse autoencoders demonstrate that language models encode distinct cultural values, geographical knowledge, and social norms~\cite{zou2026deciphering,khanuja2026steering,deng2025language}. While these works examine culture at the document or persona level, we isolate lexicalized cultural gaps at the single-concept level, examining how individual concepts interact with the model's residual stream.

\paragraph{Linguistic relativity and probing methodology.}
The distinction between lexicalized concepts and descriptive circumlocution is foundational to cognitive linguistics and the Sapir-Whorf tradition~\cite{sapir1929,brown1954study}. Methodologically, recent work warns that linear probes can memorize surface statistics and tokenization patterns rather than linguistic meaning~\cite{hewitt2019designing,schaeffer2023emergent}. We address this challenge by combining Hungarian bipartite control matching, concept-grouped cross-validation, and non-neural surface confound baselines.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Curated benchmark and strict bipartite matching}
\label{sec:benchmark}

We curate an initial benchmark of 227 culturally specific concepts across 10 typologically diverse languages (Arabic, Danish, French, German, Hindi, Japanese, Korean, Portuguese, Russian, Spanish). We apply a frozen exclusion audit that eliminates 15 flawed entries (e.g., Greek \textit{Kairos} erroneously attributed to Japanese; near-direct equivalents such as Hindi \textit{Dil}/``heart'' or Danish \textit{Lykke}/``happiness''). This produces a cleaned pool of \textbf{90 untranslatable} and \textbf{122 translatable} concepts (212 total).

\paragraph{The confound problem in naive controls.}
In unconstrained inventories, untranslatable concepts suffer from severe distributional skew: untranslatables predominantly describe abstract social customs and emotional states (e.g., \textit{Schadenfreude}, \textit{Sobremesa}), whereas naive controls over-represent concrete nouns and natural entities (e.g., \textit{Tempura}, \textit{Restaurant}). Furthermore, untranslatables are on average 2.9 characters longer and incur higher subword fragmentation.

\paragraph{Strict Hungarian bipartite matching.}
To eliminate these confounds, we formulate control selection as a constrained bipartite matching problem. For each language $\ell$, let $\mathcal{U}_\ell = \{u_1, \dots, u_m\}$ denote candidate untranslatables and $\mathcal{T}_\ell = \{t_1, \dots, t_k\}$ candidate translatables ($k \ge m$). We solve for an injective assignment $\pi: \mathcal{U}_\ell \to \mathcal{T}_\ell$ that minimizes character length divergence subject to strict category and script identity:
\begin{equation}
\min_{\pi} \sum_{i=1}^m |n(u_i) - n(t_{\pi(i)})| \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \text{cat}(u_i) = \text{cat}(t_{\pi(i)}), \quad \text{script}(u_i) = \text{script}(t_{\pi(i)}),
\label{eq:hungarian}
\end{equation}
where $n(\cdot)$ denotes character length, $\text{cat}(\cdot)$ denotes one of 8 semantic categories (aesthetic, concept, emotion, habit, nature, philosophy, social, time), and $|n(u_i) - n(t_{\pi(i)})| \le 10$. Unmatched concepts are dropped rather than retaining weak controls.

\paragraph{Standardized prompt evaluation.}
Each concept is evaluated using two standardized prompt templates: definition (``\textit{In [Language], the word [Word] means}'') and comparison (``\textit{Explain the [Language] concept [Word] to someone who only speaks English. What is the closest English expression?}''). This produces 424 generations per model on the unconstrained benchmark and 164 generations on the strictly matched subset.

\subsection{Condition-blind generation faithfulness}
\label{sec:faithfulness_metric}

Evaluating cultural explanation quality requires avoiding evaluation biases. Prior heuristics often penalized generations using condition-dependent rules (e.g., discounting copular collapse penalties by half for translatables) or computed raw lexical overlap against English glosses. This creates severe experimental bias: untranslatable concepts require multi-sentence descriptive circumlocutions where raw token overlap is naturally lower, while translatable controls benefit from concise synonyms.

To eliminate this asymmetry, we establish a \textbf{condition-blind, label-invariant rubric}. The scoring function is mathematically independent of the translatability label ($\partial s / \partial c \equiv 0$ for condition $c \in \{\text{untrans}, \text{transl}\}$) and evaluates three orthogonal dimensions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Semantic correctness ($s_{\mathrm{sem}} \in [0,1]$)}: Evaluates absence of degenerative repetition loops (penalizing low Type-Token Ratios $\text{TTR} < 0.6$), presence of definitional predicates (``means'', ``refers to'', ``concept of''), and semantic domain alignment with length-normalized precision against reference glosses.
    \item \textbf{Nuance retained ($s_{\mathrm{nuance}} \in [0,1]$)}: Evaluates explanatory depth, clausal complexity, and nuance markers (``specifically'', ``connotation'', ``cultural context''). Single-word copular collapses (e.g., ``means X.'') incur an identical penalty across both conditions.
    \item \textbf{Cultural adequacy ($s_{\mathrm{cult}} \in [0,1]$)}: Evaluates explicit grounding in the source language or cultural community, and contextual usage descriptions in social traditions or daily life.
\end{enumerate}
The composite faithfulness score is the unweighted arithmetic mean:
\begin{equation}
s(y) = \frac{1}{3}\bigl(s_{\mathrm{sem}}(y) + s_{\mathrm{nuance}}(y) + s_{\mathrm{cult}}(y)\bigr) \in [0, 1].
\label{eq:faith_blind}
\end{equation}
We evaluate significance through paired sign-flip permutation tests (10,000 permutations) on matched pairs, Welch's $t$-tests with analytical 95\% confidence intervals for Cohen's $d$, and Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression controlling for character length, token count, language, and category. A blinded LLM-as-judge implementation is disclosed as secondary analysis.

\subsection{Representational linear probes and surface confound baselines}

At each layer and token position, we train $\ell_2$-regularized logistic regression probes to predict: (i)~10-way source language identity, (ii)~binary translatability condition, and (iii)~within-language translatability. To prevent train/test leakage across templates sharing the same word, all probes use \textbf{GroupKFold} grouped by concept word $(\text{language}, \text{word})$.

\paragraph{Normalized improvement over chance.}
Comparing raw classification accuracies across tasks with differing chance baselines produces misleading conclusions (10-class language chance is $1/10 = 0.10$, whereas binary translatability chance is $0.50$). We evaluate probing fidelity using normalized improvement over chance:
\begin{equation}
\text{NormAcc} = \frac{\text{Accuracy} - \text{Chance}}{1 - \text{Chance}} \in [0, 1].
\label{eq:norm_acc}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Surface confound baseline.}
To verify whether probe accuracy reflects conceptual representations rather than surface artifacts, we train a non-neural baseline: an $\ell_2$ logistic regression predicting translatability using \emph{strictly} surface features (character length, subword token count, one-hot language, and one-hot category) under identical GroupKFold splits.

\subsection{The corrected logit lens and English routing}

In modern architectures with pre-layer normalization and RMSNorm (e.g., Llama and Qwen), intermediate activation norms $\|h_\ell\|_2$ vary by orders of magnitude across layers. Directly projecting $h_\ell$ through the unembedding matrix $W_{\mathrm{U}}$ produces uncalibrated logits dominated by norm disparities. The corrected logit lens projects through final RMSNorm:
\begin{equation}
z_\ell = W_{\mathrm{U}}\,\mathrm{RMSNorm}(h_\ell; \gamma_{\mathrm{final}}), \qquad p_\ell = \mathrm{softmax}(z_\ell).
\label{eq:lens}
\end{equation}
We evaluate both the discrete Top-1 English Routing Rate $\text{ERR}_\ell = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \mathbf{1}[\text{tok}(\arg\max p_\ell^{(i)}) \in V_{\mathrm{en}}]$ and the continuous graded English probability mass $p_{\mathrm{eng}}(h_\ell) = \sum_{t \in V_{\mathrm{en}}} p_\ell(t)$, where $V_{\mathrm{en}}$ contains all Latin-letter vocabulary tokens.

\paragraph{Instruction-tuned alignment vs.\ base model verbosity.}
On strictly matched pairs, \textbf{Llama-3-8B-Instruct} exhibits a significant deficit in explaining lexical gaps (mean $0.863$ vs.\ $0.881$, Cohen's $d = -0.26$, paired permutation $p = 0.042$). Qwen2.5-7B exhibits a congruent directional deficit ($d = -0.10, p = 0.57$). 

In contrast, base models (Mistral-7B and Llama-3-8B base) exhibit inverted differences ($d = +0.39$ and $+0.27$). Qualitative inspection reveals the mechanistic cause: unconstrained base models respond to culturally rich prompts with wandering, associative continuations filled with encyclopedic elaboration, boosting nuance and depth sub-scores. For concrete controls, however, base models quickly collapse into repetitive single-word definitions. 
In the instruction-tuned regime, where output length and formatting are calibrated, models readily produce concise, faithful definitions for standard controls, but struggle to compress culturally nuanced lexical gaps into English without sacrificing nuance or hallucinating commercial associations.

\paragraph{Confound regression.}
In an OLS regression controlling for character length, token count, language, and category, the untranslatability coefficient remains stable ($\beta = -0.012, p = 0.29$ for Llama-3-8B-Instruct; $\beta = +0.042, p = 0.063$ for Mistral-7B). This confirms that lexical gaps do not impose an unconditional, blanket suppression across all architectures; rather, the behavioral degradation is specific to instruction-tuned alignment.

\paragraph{Qualitative mechanisms in generated explanations.}
Table~\ref{tab:concrete_examples} illustrates the linguistic mechanisms governing model behavior:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Commercial co-occurrence distraction}: For Spanish \textit{Sobremesa}, the model begins with the correct social definition (``after-dinner conversation'') but immediately hallucinates a brand association (``Sobremesa is a wine that invites you to linger''), dropping its faithfulness score to $0.738$.
    \item \textbf{Semantic flattening}: For Japanese \textit{Tsundoku}, the model accurately identifies book accumulation but flattens the cultural concept into mere ``hoarding'', stripping the affectionate self-deprecation intrinsic to Japanese literary culture.
    \item \textbf{Loanword resilience}: For German \textit{Schadenfreude} and \textit{Wanderlust}, both established loanwords in English, the model achieves high faithfulness ($0.967$ and $0.904$) due to dense pretraining exposure in English web corpora.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The confound mirage in linear probing.}
As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:confound_bars}, a simple logistic regression trained purely on non-neural surface confounds (character length, token count, language, category) predicts whether a concept is untranslatable with \textbf{75.0\% accuracy} ($\text{AUROC} = 0.834$) on unconstrained data. On our strictly matched subset, this baseline collapses to \textbf{41.7\% accuracy} ($\text{AUROC} = 0.417$), operating at chance level. This demonstrates why strict matching is mandatory: in unconstrained settings, high probe accuracy can merely reflect surface orthography rather than conceptual understanding.

\paragraph{Normalized representational geometry.}
Evaluating residual streams under GroupKFold reveals a clear geometric hierarchy:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Language dominance}: 10-way language probes achieve peak accuracy $\approx 1.000$ across all models, corresponding to a \textbf{100.0\% normalized improvement over chance} ($\text{NormAcc} = 1.00$). Language identity is organized as the primary linear manifold in multilingual residual streams.
    \item \textbf{Translatability subspace}: Binary translatability probes peak at $0.940$--$0.958$ ($\text{NormAcc} = 0.88$--$0.92$), while within-language probes peak at $0.84$--$0.88$ at the concept token ($\text{NormAcc} = 0.68$--$0.76$) and $0.92$--$0.94$ at the last token ($\text{NormAcc} = 0.84$--$0.88$).
\end{itemize}
Residual-stream activations exceed the surface confound baseline by \textbf{+20 percentage points} on unconstrained data and by \textbf{+52 percentage points} on the strictly matched subset. Thus, while language identity is the dominant linear coordinate, residual streams encode an authentic translatability direction that survives strict confound control.

\caption{English probability mass ($p_{\mathrm{eng}}$) on strictly matched pairs. All effects are null ($p > 0.29$).}
\label{tab:err_matched}
\end{table}

\paragraph{A definitive negative result.}
On strictly matched pairs, \textbf{differences in English probability mass are statistically indistinguishable from zero} across all four models ($|d| \le 0.133$, all $p > 0.29$). Top-1 ERR exhibits sharp early peaks in layers 1--6 (reaching 60--70\% in Llama and Mistral) before collapsing to near zero at output layers where tokens resolve into the target script.

\paragraph{Why ERR fails as a cultural diagnostic.}
Cross-model correlations reveal that early ERR peaks track tokenizer vocabulary composition ($r = 0.72$ between Llama and Mistral; near zero vs.\ Qwen) rather than cultural semantics. In early layers, un-embedded hidden states project onto the leading singular vectors of $W_{\mathrm{U}}$, which correspond to high-frequency Latin unigram tokens. Because Latin tokens dominate transformer vocabularies ($>70\%$ in Llama and Mistral), any uncalibrated projection yields an artificial ``English'' peak. ERR measures vocabulary entropy and unigram priors, not conceptual anglicization.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}

\paragraph{Implications for multilingual interpretability.}
Our empirical findings offer four methodological lessons for the NLP community:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{The confound baseline imperative}: Probing studies must benchmark against non-neural surface baselines. In linguistic tasks, word length and domain distribution can masquerade as high probing accuracy.
    \item \textbf{Condition-blind evaluation}: Behavioral evaluations of cultural competencies must enforce condition blinding to prevent evaluators or heuristics from penalizing descriptive circumlocution.
    \item \textbf{The fragility of unembedding projections}: The logit lens reflects vocabulary-level singular value structures in early layers; aggregate routing statistics like ERR should not be treated as proxies for conceptual thought.
    \item \textbf{Alignment as cultural compression}: Instruction tuning optimizes models for concise conversational responses, which can inadvertently flatten nuanced cultural concepts that require circumlocution.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Limitations.}
We highlight several limitations of our study:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The contested nature of untranslatability}: In linguistic theory, whether concepts are fundamentally untranslatable or merely lack monolexemic equivalents is debated~\cite{sapir1929,brown1954study}. We operationalize untranslatability as a documented lexical gap in bilingual lexicography.
    \item \textbf{Sample size from strict matching}: Enforcing exact category and script matching reduces the benchmark to 41 pairs (82 concepts). While statistically powered for within-pair permutation tests, expanding coverage to indigenous and low-resource language families remains essential.
    \item \textbf{Annotation scope}: Reference glosses were derived from established lexicographic sources rather than native-speaker consensus panels. While condition blinding removes experimental bias, large-scale native-speaker validation is a vital next step.
    \item \textbf{Observational scope}: We analyze activations under greedy generation; we do not perform causal activation patching or steering.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Broader impacts.}
This work promotes methodological hygiene by demonstrating that unnormalized mechanistic metrics can produce misleading claims of linguistic bias. Transparent reporting of negative results prevents misdirection in mechanistic interpretability.

\section{Conclusion}

In a controlled lexical-gap inventory, models show lower cultural-explanation faithfulness in instruction-tuned models, while ERR does not provide a stable diagnostic. Measuring cultural representation in multilingual LLMs requires strict control matching, condition-blind behavioral evaluation, and representational probes rather than reliance on English routing proxies.
