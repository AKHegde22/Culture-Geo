\begin{abstract}
Prior work shows that multilingual LLMs often process meaning in an English-centric latent space, but almost exclusively for concepts that \emph{have} English equivalents. We study the complementary case: culturally specific concepts with no single-word English translation. Across four open-weight models (Llama-3-8B, Llama-3-8B-Instruct, Mistral-7B-v0.3, Qwen2.5-7B) and a cleaned inventory of 90 untranslatable concepts with 122 matched controls (10 languages; 636 prompts), we obtain a three-part result. \textbf{(1)~Behavioral.} Greedy generations scored with a reproducible faithfulness rubric are significantly worse for untranslatable concepts in all four models (Cohen's $d\in[-0.56,-0.39]$, all $p<10^{-4}$). \textbf{(2)~Representational.} Linear probes with concept-grouped cross-validation recover source language nearly perfectly, while translatability is weaker but above chance---including within language. \textbf{(3)~Cautionary.} English Routing Rate (ERR) shows \emph{no} consistent ``untranslatable$\to$English'' cost at the last token; early-layer peaks and tokenizer size dominate. Lexical gaps therefore leave a clearer fingerprint in \emph{how models explain culture} and \emph{where language identity lives} than in aggregate English-routing statistics.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Multilingual large language models (LLMs) are used worldwide, yet mechanistic analyses of their internals remain dominated by an English lens. Recent work argues that key semantic decisions occur in a representation space closest to English~\cite{schut2025think,wendler2024llamas}, and that romanization can bridge concept space and language-specific output~\cite{saji2025romanlens}. These studies, however, largely examine content that \emph{does} have English equivalents. A culturally consequential question remains open: what happens when a concept has \emph{no} single-word English equivalent?

Consider Japanese \textit{Tsundoku}---the habit of acquiring books and letting them pile up unread. When a model processes such a concept, three outcomes are plausible: (i)~it forces an English approximation in the residual stream; (ii)~it carves out an isolated, language-specific region; or (iii)~its \emph{explanations} of the concept degrade even when residual-stream English statistics look normal.

We test these hypotheses with three complementary measurements on a frozen, audited concept inventory:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Generation faithfulness} (primary behavioral outcome): do models produce weaker cultural explanations for untranslatable concepts than for matched controls?
    \item \textbf{Linear probes} (primary representational outcome): is \emph{translatability} linearly recoverable from activations, or primarily \emph{language identity}?
    \item \textbf{English routing} (cautionary secondary): does the logit lens show elevated English probability for lexical gaps?
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Contributions.}
(i)~We release a cleaned untranslatability probe set with matching diagnostics and an exclusion audit. (ii)~We show a consistent behavioral cost of lexical gaps under generation faithfulness across four models. (iii)~We show that residual-stream geometry encodes language strongly and translatability more weakly, while ERR---a popular English-routing summary---does not detect the gap. Together, these results reframe how cultural lexical gaps should be measured in LLMs.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{English-centric latent spaces.}
Schut et al.~\cite{schut2025think} and Wendler et al.~\cite{wendler2024llamas} provide evidence that multilingual transformers route semantically loaded content through English-adjacent representations. RomanLens~\cite{saji2025romanlens} shows that intermediate layers often prefer romanized forms before resolving to native script. We extend this line of work to the lexical-gap regime and argue that ERR alone is a brittle primary metric for cultural claims.

\paragraph{Cultural representations.}
Sparse-feature analyses of cultural~\cite{zou2026deciphering,khanuja2026steering} and language-specific~\cite{deng2025language} structure suggest that LLMs encode culture and language as partially separable features. We ask a sharper question: whether \emph{absent} English equivalents---not just culture-tagged concepts---change generation quality or linear geometry.

\paragraph{Untranslatability.}
Lexical gaps are classical objects in linguistic relativity~\cite{sapir1929}. We operationalize them with a generation rubric, concept-grouped linear probes, and corrected logit-lens diagnostics.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Dataset}

We start from 227 culturally specific concepts across 10 languages (Arabic, Danish, German, Spanish, French, Hindi, Japanese, Korean, Portuguese, Russian) and apply a frozen audit that drops clearly invalid ``untranslatable'' labels (e.g., Greek \textit{Kairos} mislabeled as Japanese; near-direct glosses such as Hindi \textit{Dil}/``heart''). The cleaned set contains \textbf{90 untranslatable} and \textbf{122 translatable} controls (212 concepts). Matching diagnostics record language$\times$category coverage, character-length summaries, English-loanword flags, and greedy same-language control pairs.

Each concept is rendered with three prompt templates---definition, usage, and comparison---yielding 636 prompts. Generation experiments use definition and comparison (424 generations per model).

We study four open-weight models (Table~\ref{tab:models}). For every prompt we record residual-stream activations at (i)~the last non-padding token and (ii)~the first token of the concept word, together with LM-head weights and the final RMSNorm gain required for a correct logit lens.

\subsection{Generation faithfulness}

For each concept we generate a greedy continuation ($T{=}0$, $\mathrm{max\_new\_tokens}{=}128$) for definition and comparison prompts. Let $y$ be the generation, $m$ the gold English gloss, $w$ the concept word, $\ell$ the source language, and $c\in\{\mathrm{untrans},\mathrm{transl}\}$ the label. Writing $T(\cdot)$ for the set of alphabetic tokens (lowercased) and $n{=}|y|$ for the whitespace token count, meaning overlap is
\begin{equation}
s_{\mathrm{mean}}(y,m)
=\min\!\bigl(1,\,1.5\cdot |T(y)\cap T(m)|/|T(m)|\bigr).
\label{eq:mean}
\end{equation}
Cultural specificity accumulates additive rewards (clipped to $[0,1]$):
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
s_{\mathrm{spec}}
=\mathrm{clip}_{[0,1]}\bigl(
&0.25\cdot\mathbf{1}[w\subseteq y]
+0.15\cdot\mathbf{1}[\ell\subseteq y]\\
&+0.30\cdot\mathbf{1}[n{\ge}12]
+0.15\cdot\mathbf{1}[n{\ge}25]
+0.15\cdot\mathbf{1}[\text{punct.\ in }y]
\bigr),
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:spec}
\end{equation}
and English-collapse penalties accumulate as
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\tilde s_{\mathrm{coll}}
&=\mathrm{clip}_{[0,1]}\bigl(
0.5\cdot\mathbf{1}[n{\le}4]
+0.2\cdot\mathbf{1}[n{\le}8]
+0.3\cdot\mathbf{1}[\text{gloss pattern}]
\bigr),\\
s_{\mathrm{coll}}
&=\begin{cases}
0.5\,\tilde s_{\mathrm{coll}} & \text{if }c=\mathrm{transl},\\
\tilde s_{\mathrm{coll}} & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:coll}
\end{equation}
where ``gloss pattern'' matches short templates such as ``means $X$'' / ``is $X$'' / ``refers to $X$''. The composite faithfulness score is
\begin{equation}
s(y)=\mathrm{clip}_{[0,1]}\!\bigl(
0.45\,s_{\mathrm{mean}}+0.40\,s_{\mathrm{spec}}-0.25\,s_{\mathrm{coll}}+0.20
\bigr).
\label{eq:faith}
\end{equation}
We compare untranslatable vs.\ translatable scores with Welch's $t$-test and Cohen's $d$. The rubric is fully deterministic for reproducibility; an LLM-as-judge interface is provided in code as a drop-in replacement.

\subsection{Linear probes}

At layer $\ell$ and token position $p\in\{\mathrm{last},\mathrm{concept}\}$ we fit $\ell_2$-regularized logistic regression probes to predict language identity or the binary translatability label. To prevent leakage across the three templates that share a concept, we use \textbf{GroupKFold} with groups defined by $(\mathrm{language},\mathrm{word})$. We additionally fit within-language translatability probes that remove the language confound. Let $\mathrm{Acc}_{\mathrm{lang}}(\ell)$ and $\mathrm{Acc}_{\mathrm{tr}}(\ell)$ denote mean CV accuracy; the quantity of interest is the gap
\begin{equation}
\Delta(\ell)=\mathrm{Acc}_{\mathrm{lang}}(\ell)-\mathrm{Acc}_{\mathrm{tr}}(\ell).
\label{eq:gap}
\end{equation}

\subsection{English routing (secondary)}

The corrected logit lens projects a hidden state $h_\ell$ through final RMSNorm and the LM head:
\begin{equation}
z_\ell = W_{\mathrm{U}}\,\mathrm{RMSNorm}(h_\ell),\qquad
p_\ell=\mathrm{softmax}(z_\ell).
\label{eq:lens}
\end{equation}
Top-1 English Routing Rate at layer $\ell$ is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{ERR}_\ell
=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}\!\bigl[\mathrm{tok}(\arg\max p_\ell^{(i)})\in V_{\mathrm{en}}\bigr],
\label{eq:err}
\end{equation}
and the graded English probability mass is
\begin{equation}
p_{\mathrm{eng}}(h_\ell)=\sum_{t\in V_{\mathrm{en}}} p_\ell(t),
\label{eq:peng}
\end{equation}
where $V_{\mathrm{en}}$ is the set of lowercase Latin-letter vocabulary tokens (after stripping BPE spacing markers). Omitting final RMSNorm invalidates the projection for some architectures (notably Qwen); we therefore always apply it.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Generation: a behavioral cost of lexical gaps}

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figures/cross_model_faithfulness.pdf}
    \caption{Overall generation faithfulness by model and group (higher is better).}
    \label{fig:faith}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:faith} and Table~\ref{tab:faith} summarize $s(y)$ on 424 generations per model. \textbf{In all four models, untranslatable concepts receive significantly lower faithfulness} ($d\in[-0.56,-0.39]$, all $p<10^{-4}$). The effect is strongest on definition prompts ($d\in[-0.85,-0.67]$, all $p<10^{-5}$); comparison prompts show smaller gaps that remain significant in three of four models. This is precisely the behavioral signature that last-token ERR does not reveal: models explain lexical-gap concepts less faithfully than matched controls.

\subsection{Probes: language dominates, translatability is weaker}

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figures/llama-3-8b_probe_concept_token.pdf}
    \caption{Concept-token probe accuracy for language vs.\ translatability (Llama-3-8B; GroupKFold by concept). Chance is $0.1$ for language and $0.5$ for translatability.}
    \label{fig:probes}
\end{figure}

GroupKFold probes recover source language nearly perfectly at both positions (peak $\mathrm{Acc}_{\mathrm{lang}}\approx 1.0$; chance $0.1$). Translatability accuracy is high but strictly lower (peak $\mathrm{Acc}_{\mathrm{tr}}\approx 0.94$--$0.96$), and within-language peaks remain above chance ($\approx 0.84$--$0.94$). Thus $\Delta(\ell)>0$ consistently: language identity is the dominant linear structure, yet a residual lexical-gap direction exists that ERR fails to surface. Concept-token language ARI (Figure~\ref{fig:ari_overlay}) likewise rises far above last-token ARI in late layers (peaks up to $\sim$0.85 for Llama-3-8B-Instruct).

    \centering

    \caption{Language ARI across layers at last-token vs.\ concept-token positions (Llama-3-8B).}
    \label{fig:ari_overlay}
\end{figure}

\subsection{English routing: a null secondary finding}

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figures/cross_model_err.pdf}
    \caption{English Routing Rate (Eq.~\ref{eq:err}) across layers (untranslatable solid, translatable dashed).}
    \label{fig:err_all}
\end{figure}

ERR is highest in early layers---where top-1 predictions reflect embedding/unembedding geometry and high-frequency English tokens---and collapses at the output, where models predict source-language continuations (Figure~\ref{fig:err_all}). Layer-wise Welch tests find \emph{no} significant untranslatable$>$translatable ERR difference in any model (Table~\ref{tab:err}); trends often point the other way. Graded mid-layer $p_{\mathrm{eng}}$ is likewise null in three of four models at the last token. Concept-token $p_{\mathrm{eng}}$ effects that do reach significance flip sign across architectures (Llama-3-8B $d{=}+0.27$; Qwen2.5-7B $d{=}{-}0.45$), so they cannot support a unified English-routing account of untranslatability.

Cross-model ERR correlations are high within Llama/Mistral ($r\approx 0.56$--$0.72$) but near zero or negative vs.\ Qwen, tracking tokenizer vocabulary structure rather than shared cultural semantics. Sparse dictionary features ($k{=}512$) likewise fail to separate groups better than chance at either token position.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{Measure what culture does, not only where English peaks.}
Generation faithfulness and concept-token probes speak more directly to cultural representation than aggregate ERR. The null ERR result is informative: it warns against reading early-layer English mass as evidence of cultural bias.

\paragraph{Language identity dominates residual geometry.}
Near-perfect language probes and high concept-token ARI show that models encode \emph{which language a word belongs to}. Within-language translatability probes above chance further suggest a residual lexical-gap correlate that does not appear as elevated English routing---a dissociation between probe geometry and logit-lens ERR.

\paragraph{Tokenizer and early-layer effects dominate routing summaries.}
Practical recommendations follow: always apply final RMSNorm in logit-lens analyses; discard early and output layers when summarizing routing; report tokenizer diagnostics alongside ERR; and prefer graded and behavioral metrics.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
The cleaned inventory is still modest relative to conversational cultural use. The faithfulness score in Eq.~\ref{eq:faith} is a reproducible lexical/structural proxy---not a human or LLM judge---and may under-penalize fluent but culturally shallow text; LLM-as-judge and human spot-checks are natural extensions. Single-concept prompts omit dialogue context. We do not run causal interventions (activation patching or steering).

\paragraph{Broader impacts.}
Positive: clearer metrics can reduce over-claiming of ``English bias'' from routing artifacts and encourage culturally faithful evaluation. Negative: lexical-gap inventories can encode annotator subjectivity and Western-centric notions of what counts as ``untranslatable''; generation evaluations could be misused to rank languages by stereotyped ``difficulty.'' We mitigate by releasing the exclusion audit, matching diagnostics, and deterministic rubric so assumptions are inspectable.

\paragraph{Compute.}
Activation extraction and generation used NVIDIA L4 GPUs via Modal; full Path-B analysis for four models required on the order of a few GPU-hours. Code, cleaned data, probe results, and generations are released with the paper repository.

\section{Conclusion}

Untranslatable cultural concepts do not leave a reliable last-token English-routing signature, yet they are not invisible: language identity is strongly recoverable at the concept token, within-language probes detect a weaker lexical-gap direction, and generated explanations are consistently less faithful for untranslatable concepts than for matched controls across four models. Measuring generation quality and concept-token geometry therefore complements---and corrects---over-reliance on ERR alone.
